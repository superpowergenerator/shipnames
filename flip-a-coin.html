How to get the most out of this game

Write your two options before you flip, and make them both real choices you'd enjoy. If heads means a movie night and tails means a walk in the park, either result leaves you happy, and the flip feels like a gift instead of a gamble. The game works best when losing the toss still wins you a good night. A flip a coin toss only feels fair when both sides are worth landing on.

Say your pick out loud before the coin lands, and watch what you hope for. If you call heads and then feel a pang when it lands tails, you've just learned what you really wanted. That tiny moment of honesty is the secret power of a coin toss, and it tells you more than the result does.

Use one flip per decision, and stick to it unless you both agree to change it. A single toss ends the debate in seconds, which is the whole point, while a second toss just restarts the argument you were trying to end. If the result feels wrong to both of you, that's useful news too, since it means neither option was right.

Keep the coin handy for small choices all week, not just tonight. Who picks the next song, which cafe to try, or what to cook on Friday all work as coin flips, and each one saves you a small debate. Tiny decisions add up to a lot of wasted time, so letting chance handle them frees you both up for the choices that matter. What to do after the game

Do what the coin says right away after a flip a coin toss, while the energy is still there. If it lands on heads and heads means you pick the movie, start the movie now instead of debating the pick all over again. A fast follow-through makes the flip feel real, and it teaches you both to trust the result.

Note the choices where you both secretly hoped for the same side. If you both wanted heads, that's a clue about what you both enjoy, and it's worth remembering for next time. You can explore those shared tastes further on the couple activities to do together page, which lists things you can plan as a pair.

Turn a fun flip into a small tradition for recurring choices. If the coin picks your Friday dinner spot each week, the toss itself becomes part of the ritual, and you'll both look forward to it. Traditions like that cost nothing and give ordinary weeks a little spark.

When the coin has settled your choice, try a game that needs more than luck to round out the night. The rock paper scissors game adds a little skill and a best-of-5 match, with the winner picking the next date idea. You'll find both games on the couple games page, and pairing a quick flip with a longer game gives you a full evening in minutes.

Mistakes to avoid

A flip a coin toss can't decide a choice that actually matters to one of you. A coin can't decide where to live, how to spend real money, or whether to take a big trip, and letting it try will leave someone feeling unheard. The flip stays fun only when the stakes stay low.

Reflipping until you get the answer you wanted just means you're picking with extra steps. Best of three, best of five, and one more for luck all defeat the point, since you're just picking with extra steps. If you catch yourself asking for another toss, stop and admit you already know what you want. Honesty beats another flip, and your partner will respect the direct answer more.

A flip where one side is clearly a punishment isn't a fair toss. Heads means a nice dinner while tails means doing the dishes isn't a fair toss, and it turns a game into a trap. Both sides should feel like a treat, or at least like a fair share of a chore. If you can't make both sides pleasant, the choice isn't a coin flip, it's a chore chart, so handle it that way instead.

Dodging a talk you've been putting off is a job for words, not a coin. Flipping to decide who brings up a hard subject just delays it, and the delay usually makes it harder. Put the coin down, pick a calm moment, and say what needs saying in your own words.
